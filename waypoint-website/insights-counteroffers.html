Before you accept a counteroffer: a guide for engineers

Waypoint Search Group · 5 min read

You accepted a new offer and gave notice. Then your manager came back with a counteroffer: more money, maybe a new title, maybe the project you've been asking for. It's flattering, and it's meant to be. Before you decide, step back and look at the whole picture.

Start with why you started looking

Write down the reasons you were open to a move in the first place. Maybe it was compensation, but it was rarely only compensation. It might have been the type of projects, a stalled path to PM or principal, a lack of mentorship, a long commute or a culture that wasn't working.

Then ask honestly: does the counteroffer fix those things, or does it mainly fix the number?

Ask what changed

If a raise or promotion was possible now, it's fair to ask why it wasn't offered before you resigned. Sometimes the answer is reasonable, such as a review cycle that was already planned. Sometimes it signals that recognition at your firm comes only when someone is about to leave.

Consider how the relationship may shift

Your leadership now knows you were ready to go. Many people stay after a counteroffer and do well. Still, it's worth thinking about how that knowledge might affect trust, project assignments and succession plans, and whether you'd feel comfortable raising concerns in the future.

Compare the full picture side by side

Put both opportunities in one table and compare more than salary:

  • Total compensation, including bonus, benefits and license or education support
  • The projects and clients you'd actually work on over the next two years
  • Your path to the next title, and who decides it
  • Mentorship, team stability and leadership you trust
  • Flexibility, commute and time in the field

Think about your reputation

Architecture, engineering and construction communities are close-knit, especially within a region or a specialty. Backing out of an accepted offer is sometimes the right call, but do it with care. Be prompt, direct and respectful with the firm you're turning down.

When staying can make sense

If the counteroffer genuinely resolves the reasons you were leaving — a defined role change, new responsibilities, a different team — staying may be the right decision. Get the specifics in writing, including timelines for any promised promotion or review.

Talk it through

A good recruiter should help you think clearly, not just push you toward the new role. We'll always give you our honest view, even when that means staying where you are.
